01 — What Eberron Is (and Isn't)

Eberron is pulp noir meets wide magic. Think Indiana Jones with wands, Casablanca in a city lit by bound elementals, hardboiled detectives chasing shapeshifters through a train powered by lightning. It is emphatically not Tolkien pastoral fantasy.

Magic is common but mastery is rare. A farmer might own a continual flame lantern; that doesn't mean they can cast a single spell. Airships cross the sky. Newspapers run off printing presses enchanted with prestidigitation. Healing potions are sold in corner shops. But an archmage is still extraordinary.

Morality is grey by design. The paladin's church might be corrupt. The necromancer might run an orphanage. Alignment exists mechanically but the setting actively undermines "detect evil and smite" gameplay.

Player Note Build a character with debts, secrets, and opinions — not just a class and a backstory paragraph. Eberron's noir tone rewards characters who are entangled with the world, not passing through it.
Player Note — Forget Generic Fantasy Leave your Forgotten Realms assumptions at the door. In Eberron, a halfling barbarian rides a dinosaur into town and nobody blinks. Elves practise ancestor worship, not woodland mysticism. Goblins built a continent-spanning empire thousands of years before humans learned to write. Orcs were the original defenders against extraplanar invasion. If your character concept relies on a fantasy stereotype, check whether Eberron subverts it — it probably does.

Species in Eberron — Everyone's Here, Everyone's Different

Baker's design rule: "If it exists in D&D, it has a place in Eberron." Every species has been recontextualised for a world of industrialised magic, continental war, and moral complexity. No species is inherently evil.

Designer's Intent — No Inherently Evil Species Eberron doesn't do inherently evil species. Goblins, orcs, drow — all complex cultures. There are evil groups (the Trust, the Emerald Claw), but no mortal species is born evil. This was radical when Eberron launched in 2004 and remains the setting's moral foundation.

02 — Core Setting Philosophy

Player Note Your character lives in a world that feels like the 1920s—but with magic instead of technology. Intrigue, moral grey areas, and consequences matter more than dungeon crawling.
Designer's Intent Eberron was born from a WotC contest — over 11,000 one-page submissions, and Keith Baker's won. He built it around four maxims: (1) If it exists in D&D, it has a place in Eberron. (2) Tone is grey, never black & white. (3) The Last War shaped everything. (4) Dragonmark dynasties are the real power.
Designer's Intent — "In My Eberron" Baker deliberately left Eberron's biggest mysteries unanswered. His mantra is "in my Eberron" — canon is a starting point, not a cage. What caused the Mourning? Where do warforged souls come from? Baker has no official answers, and that's by design. He wants your table's answer to be the real one. Even Baker himself ignores sourcebooks that don't fit his vision. Every DM is the canon authority for their own game.
Designer's Intent — The Core Theme Is Grief Look past the pulp and the noir and you find Eberron's real heart: loss, and what you build after it. Cyre — the richest, most beautiful nation — is gone. Warforged were built for a war that ended, and now they have no purpose. The Last War took a generation from every family on the continent. Eberron's most powerful emotional current isn't cynicism or adventure — it's the question of how you rebuild when the thing you loved is destroyed. The nostalgia for what Galifar was, the grief for what Cyre became, the stubborn act of making something new from the wreckage — that's the setting's soul.

Eberron rewards investigation, intrigue, and moral ambiguity over dungeon-clearing. NPCs have competing agendas. Nations are in cold war. Players should regularly face situations where the "right" answer isn't obvious—and might not exist at all.

This doesn't mean you can't run a dungeon crawl. Xen'drik is right there, full of ancient giant ruins. But even a dungeon should echo the wider tensions: who hired you, and what do they really want with that artifact?

03 — The Three Dragons & the Planes

Chapter Summary Three Progenitor Dragons created the world and became it: Siberys (the sky), Eberron (the land), Khyber (the underworld). Their conflict explains dragonshards, the Draconic Prophecy, and why fiends are imprisoned below. Thirteen planes orbit the material world, bleeding through in manifest zones that shape geography and adventure sites.

Eberron has an origin myth older than the Age of Monsters, and it shapes everything. Three Progenitor Dragons created reality, and their bodies became reality.

The Progenitor Dragons

Three dragons shaped the world together. Then Khyber betrayed and murdered Siberys. Eberron wrapped her own body around Khyber to imprison her forever. Their bodies became the cosmos:

DragonBecameAssociated With
SiberysThe Dragon Above — the ring of golden dragonshards orbiting the planetDragonmarks, celestial power, the heavens
EberronThe Dragon Between — the living world itselfNature, the material plane, primal magic
KhyberThe Dragon Below — the underworld, imprisoned within EberronFiends, aberrations, the Overlords, darkness

The myth has teeth. The Progenitors explain why dragonshards exist (crystallised blood of each dragon), why the Draconic Prophecy is written into the world's geology, and why fiends are trapped beneath your feet. Every sealed Overlord, every manifest zone, every aberration crawling up from the depths ties back to these three.

DM Advice You don't need to lecture players on the creation myth. Let it surface naturally: a scholar mentions Siberys shards, a cultist invokes Khyber's name, an ancient mural depicts three intertwined serpents. The myth works best when it's ambient worldbuilding, not an opening crawl.

The Thirteen Planes

The Progenitors created thirteen planes—each an idealised expression of a single concept. These planes orbit the material world, waxing and waning in influence. Where they press close, manifest zones form: permanent patches of planar bleed-through that define Eberron's geography. Sharn's impossible towers exist because a Syrania zone makes things lighter there. A village near a Mabar zone has undead crawling from the soil every new moon.

Designer's Intent — Planes Are Concepts, Not Destinations Baker designed each plane as an idealised expression of a single idea, not as a place to visit. Fernia isn't "the fire dimension"—it's the concept of fire made manifest: industry, passion, destruction, the forge. A Fernian volcano is a metaphor. Thelanis isn't fairyland—it's the plane of stories, where narrative has literal power. Xoriat isn't just "madness"—it's the alien and unknowable, the questions you shouldn't ask; madness is merely a consequence of contact. The material plane is the nexus where all thirteen concepts overlap and interact—the only place where fire and ice, war and peace, story and stagnation coexist. This is why manifest zones are so powerful as a DM tool: you're not importing a "plane effect," you're letting a concept bleed into reality.
DM Note — Manifest Zones as Adventure Modifiers Manifest zones are your best tool for making places feel distinctive without inventing new mechanics. A tavern in a Thelanis zone where every lie turns your drink sour. A battlefield on a Shavarath zone where initiative bonuses spike and nobody can back down. Pick a zone, pick a quirk, and suddenly a random location has personality. Some concrete examples: a Thelanis town where folk wear their clothes inside-out to ward off bad luck. A Dolurrh village where spirits wander the streets at 3 a.m. A Daanvi settlement where breaking a law causes the consequences to happen to you immediately. A Fernian forge-town running a gigantic mega-foundry with no idea what to do with the overproduction. A Risian hamlet that hasn't changed in two thousand years. A Kythri island where you wake up subtly different each day. A Lamannia forest where wolves are triple-sized but the timber is hard as mithril.
DM Note — Coterminous & Remote Planes aren't static—they orbit, waxing and waning. When a plane goes coterminous (closest approach), its influence surges across all its manifest zones. When it goes remote (farthest), its influence fades. Use this mid-adventure to flip the table: Mabar goes coterminous and suddenly radiant damage is halved, healing spells heal half, light sources dim, and shadowy areas can become gateways to Mabar itself. A routine dungeon crawl becomes a survival horror overnight—and nobody did anything wrong, the cosmology shifted. Similarly, Mournland fog occasionally drifts beyond the borders, carrying mutated creatures that have no earthly reason to exist where they appear. It's a great excuse to drop any monster stat block you've been itching to use.
Player Note If your character is from a region with a strong manifest zone, it probably shaped who they are. Growing up near a Mabar zone means you've seen ghosts since childhood. A Fernia zone village means you understand fire the way coastal people understand tides. Use it.
Languages — Quick Reference Common is the trade tongue. Other languages worth knowing: Goblin (the oldest written language on Khorvaire — predates human literacy by millennia), Draconic (the language of prophecy and magic), Quori (spoken by kalashtar and the Inspired), Riedran (Sarlona's common tongue), and Giant (essential for Xen'drik ruins). Elven, Dwarven, Gnomish, and Orc round out the major living languages.

04 — History at a Glance

Chapter Summary Goblinoids ruled Khorvaire for millennia before humans arrived. Galifar I united the Five Nations ~1,000 years ago. That kingdom shattered into a century-long civil war (894–996 YK). The war ended when an unknown catastrophe annihilated Cyre in a single day. The peace is two years old and nobody trusts it.

You don't need to memorise ten thousand years. You need four beats—then some colour.

Distant Past
The world was ruled by dragons, then by giants on the continent of Xen'drik, then by goblinoids (the Dhakaani Empire) on Khorvaire. Humans arrived relatively late. All previous empires fell—violently.
c. 1 YK (~1,000 years ago)
Galifar I united the Five Nations of Khorvaire into a single kingdom. The YK calendar counts from this event. Centuries of relative peace, prosperity, and dragonmark houses rising as economic superpowers.
894–996 YK
The Last War. A century of continental war after the king died and succession was disputed. Every nation fought everyone. Warforged were mass-produced as soldiers. Magic was weaponised at industrial scale.
994 YK — The Mourning
Cyre was annihilated in a single day by an unknown magical catastrophe. No one knows the cause. The nation is now the Mournland—a warped wasteland where magic behaves unpredictably, healing fails, and living spells roam free. This trauma forced the Treaty of Thronehold (996 YK) and an uneasy peace.

The Age of Monsters

Before humans set foot on Khorvaire, the continent belonged to the Dhakaan. The goblinoid empire—hobgoblins as its generals, bugbears as its shock troops, goblins as its labour—spanned the entire landmass for thousands of years. They had architecture, magic, bardic traditions, standing armies. They were not savages. They were the civilisation.

Before them: the giants of Xen'drik, who enslaved elves and built wonders that make anything on Khorvaire look like a shed. Before even them: the dragons of Argonnessen, still alive, still watching, still older than memory. Each age ended in catastrophe—the giants were shattered by the dragons for misusing planar magic, the Dhakaani were broken by an invasion from Xoriat, the Realm of Madness, whose daelkyr drove the empire into civil war and ruin.

Designer's Intent — The Dhakaani Aren't Orcs with a Label In Exploring Eberron, Baker rebuilt the Dhakaani from the ground up as a genuinely alien civilisation. Goblinoids are eusocial—they feel a biological, psychological bond to each other that humans simply don't have. Their empire ran on this: a shared dreamspace called the Uul Dhakaan, crafted by the legendary dirge singer Jhazaal Dhakaan, guided sleeping goblinoids toward a collective destiny. Their honour system revolves around muut (duty to the group) and atcha (personal excellence in service of that duty). They're not "evil humanoids"—they're a martial culture with five millennia of history, brutalist architecture, bardic magic, and a legitimate grievance against every nation currently squatting on their ancestral land. The Heirs of Dhakaan aren't villains; they're a civilisation trying to come home.
DM Note — Running Dhakaani Ruins Dhakaani ruins are everywhere beneath Khorvaire's cities — Sharn is built on top of one. The architecture is brutalist and geometric: hexagonal corridors wide enough for bugbear formations, vaulted chambers designed for bardic magic, walls carved with Goblin script still radiating faint abjuration. The oldest levels are warped by Xoriat's lingering taint — corridors that loop, rooms where gravity shifts, symbionts fused to walls. Loot isn't gold; it's Dhakaani artefacts: singing blades, war-masks granting tactical telepathy, seal-stones keeping something below from getting out. The Heirs of Dhakaan want those artefacts back, and they have legitimate grievances and impressive discipline. Use them as rivals, employers, or both.

The Kingdom of Galifar

When humans arrived on Khorvaire, they spent centuries squabbling in small kingdoms until Galifar ir'Wynarn did what nobody thought possible: he united the Five Nations—Aundair, Breland, Cyre, Karrnath, and Thrane—under a single crown. The deal was elegant: each of his five children would govern one nation, and the eldest would inherit the throne. For nearly a thousand years, this worked.

During the Galifar era, the dragonmark houses transformed from powerful families into continent-spanning monopolies. House Cannith industrialised magical manufacturing. House Orien built the Lightning Rail. House Sivis created a communication network. The houses were officially barred from holding political office or land (the Korth Edicts), but everyone understood that economic power was the real power. The kingdom prospered, and the houses grew fat.

Player Note Your grandparents remember the golden age. Your parents remember the war. If you're under twenty, you remember nothing but aftermath. That generational divide shapes every family and every tavern conversation.

The Last War

King Jarot died in 894 YK. His children could not agree on succession. What started as a political dispute escalated into the worst conflict in Khorvaire's history—a hundred years of total war.

Every nation fought every other nation at some point. Alliances shifted constantly. The dragonmark houses officially remained neutral, selling weapons and services to all sides, and grew richer than ever. House Cannith created the warforged in 965 YK—living constructs built to fight and die so fewer humans had to. Six-foot frames of wood, stone, metal, and darkwood; hinged jawplates, crystal eyes, each stamped with a unique ghulra rune on the forehead. They don't eat, drink, breathe, or sleep. They were tools. The Treaty of Thronehold granted them legal personhood and banned creation of new warforged—but two years of freedom doesn't erase thirty years of being owned. Karrnath raised undead legions. Aundair deployed arcane superweapons. Breland invested in espionage. Thrane consolidated under the Church of the Silver Flame. Cyre, once the cultural heart of Galifar, was squeezed from all sides.

An entire generation was born, lived, and died knowing nothing but war. Every NPC over the age of twenty has war stories. Every nation has grudges. Every veteran carries scars—physical or otherwise. This is the emotional bedrock of every Eberron campaign.

Player Note — Read This First The Last War is not backstory. It's the defining fact of your character's life. You fought in it, fled from it, profited from it, or grew up in its shadow. Before you pick a class or a subclass, answer this: what did the war do to you? That answer will drive more roleplay than anything on your character sheet.
DM Note The Last War is your best tool for instant characterisation. Ask every player: what did your character do during the war? A warforged was built for it. A Cyran refugee lost everything. A Karrnathi soldier served alongside skeletons. These are live wires.

The Day of Mourning

Section Summary In 994 YK, an unknown catastrophe annihilated the entire nation of Cyre in a single day. The cause is deliberately left open — eight popular theories are listed below, all equally valid. The resulting Mournland is a dead-magic wasteland where healing fails and living spells roam free. The fear of a second Mourning is the only thing keeping the peace.

On 20 Olarune, 994 YK, Cyre simply died. A wall of dead-grey mist rolled across the nation in hours. Everything inside was annihilated, warped, or made wrong. Armies from four nations were fighting on Cyran soil that day. None of them came back. When the mist settled, it didn't dissipate—it became the permanent border of the Mournland, a zone where the rules of magic and nature no longer apply.

No one knows the cause. Was it a Cyran superweapon that backfired? A dragonmark house experiment gone wrong? A planar breach? An act of the Draconic Prophecy? The setting deliberately refuses to answer. What matters is the fear: every nation looked at the Mournland and thought, that could have been us. Two years later, the Treaty of Thronehold was signed. Not because anyone wanted peace—but because everyone was terrified.

Player Note Nobody knows what caused the Mourning. Your character has a theory — everyone does. It's probably wrong, but it shapes how you see the world.
DM Note — The Mourning The Mourning is Eberron's greatest mystery by design. Canon deliberately provides no answer. You get to decide — or never reveal it. It functions as the setting's nuclear deterrent: no nation dares reignite total war because it might happen again. Some DMs pick one theory below. Some never decide. Both work. The fear is the point, not the answer.
Designer's Intent — Why There's No Answer Baker had no explanation for the Mourning when he created the setting—he had half a dozen that all worked, and deliberately chose none. The cause was always less important to him than the impact: what it did to the people, the politics, and the peace. This is the "in my Eberron" philosophy at its most radical—the setting's most important event is a blank for you to fill.

Adventuring into the Mournland is like stepping into a grave of time. The dead-grey mist clings to your lungs and muffles sound. Spells misfire. Wounds refuse to heal. War machines rust in place while living spells wander the blasted landscape. The only things that move with ease are constructs and the undead: warforged and necromancers can cross the border unscathed. Explorers tell stories of glass forests, melted cities, and trains frozen mid-derailment. Encourage players to bring spare spellbooks and backup plans.

05 — The Last War

Chapter Summary A succession dispute in 894 YK spiralled into a hundred years of total war. Five nations, shifting alliances, warforged soldiers, undead legions, arcane superweapons. Cyre was destroyed by the Mourning in 994 YK. The Treaty of Thronehold (996 YK) ended the fighting but not the grudges. This chapter covers the full timeline, each nation's war role, character hooks, and the post-war tensions that drive every campaign.
Player Note This is the chapter that matters most for character backstories. The Last War shaped every living person on Khorvaire. Your character was born into it, fought in it, profited from it, or fled from it. Most humans know nothing else. Long-lived races have it worse — an elf who remembers centuries of peace watched it all burn.

Why It Started

King Jarot ir'Wynarn died in 894 YK. By tradition, his eldest daughter Mishann of Cyre should have inherited the throne. Her siblings disagreed. Thalin (Thrane), Kaius I (Karrnath), and Wroann (Breland) each claimed legitimacy. Wrogar (Aundair) backed Mishann—at first. What began as a succession crisis became open warfare within a year. By 896 YK, every nation was fighting at least two others.

The war was never a clean two-sided affair. Alliances formed, broke, and reformed constantly. Nations that were allies one decade invaded each other the next. This is important: every nation wronged every other nation at some point. There are no clean hands.

Timeline of Key Events

894 YK
King Jarot dies. Succession disputed. War begins between the Five Nations.
895–903 YK — Early Sieges
Breland, Thrane, and Karrnath besiege the Cyran port of Eston—twice. Both sieges fail, partly thanks to House Cannith's fortifications, partly because the attackers can't stop fighting each other.
897 YK — Karrnath's Bargain
Famine and plague ravage Karrnath. King Kaius I turns to the Blood of Vol for aid. The price: he is turned into a vampire. Karrnath begins raising undead soldiers—skeletons and zombies bolstered by necromantic magic. Effective, but it horrifies the other nations and ruins diplomacy for decades.
914 YK — Thrane's Theocracy
King Thalin of Thrane dies. The Church of the Silver Flame assumes control. Thrane becomes a theocracy under the Keeper of the Flame. The monarchy is sidelined. This changes Thrane's war aims from dynastic to ideological.
~920s–950s YK — Thrane vs. Karrnath
The theocratic Thrane targets Karrnath's undead legions specifically, framing the war as a holy struggle. Silver Flame paladins consider the undead an abomination. The conflict becomes ideological as well as territorial, and the grudge between these two nations runs deepest.
956 YK — Valenar Secession
Elf mercenaries from Aerenal, hired by Cyre to fight, decide they'd rather keep the land. They carve out Valenar from southeastern Cyre. Cyre, already losing on every front, can't stop them.
958 YK — Eldeen Reaches
Western Aundair, neglected while its armies fight elsewhere, declares independence as the Eldeen Reaches under the Great Druid Oalian and the Wardens of the Wood. Aundair loses a third of its territory overnight.
965 YK — Warforged
House Cannith perfects the modern warforged—living constructs purpose-built for combat. Mass production begins in the creation forges of Cyre. Within a decade, warforged are fighting on every front for every nation that can afford them.
969 YK — Darguun
Hobgoblin mercenary leader Lhesh Haruuc unites the goblinoid forces fighting for Cyre and Breland, turns on both, and claims a swathe of southern Cyre as the new nation of Darguun. Cyre is now being dismembered by its own hired soldiers.
976 YK — Order of the Emerald Claw
Regent Moranna of Karrnath outlaws the Order of the Emerald Claw, a fanatical military order tied to the Blood of Vol. They go underground and become one of Eberron's most persistent villain factions.
994 YK — The Day of Mourning
On 20 Olarune, Cyre is annihilated. Armies from four nations are fighting on Cyran soil that day. None survive. The dead-grey mist settles into the permanent border of the Mournland.
996 YK — Treaty of Thronehold
Two years of terror-fuelled negotiation. The treaty recognises the independence of all current nations (including Darguun, Valenar, the Eldeen Reaches, and the Mournland as unclaimed territory). Warforged are granted legal personhood. Creation of new warforged is banned. Nobody is happy. The war is over. The campaign starts here.
Player Note If you're playing a warforged, pick a creation date from this timeline. You have no childhood — just a first activation and a war you were built to fight. Two years of legal personhood doesn't erase thirty years of being property.

DM Advice — Selling the Magitech The trick is casual magic. Don't narrate it like it's special—narrate it like it's Tuesday. It rains and everyone pulls out enchanted dry brooches instead of umbrellas. City guards calmly wave away a minor illusion with practiced boredom. A bar brawl in Lower Dura escalates when the thugs pull out combat cantrips—because in Eberron, even low-level criminals have access to basic magic. The mundane made magical—and the magical made mundane—is the heartbeat of the setting.
Player Note — Magic Is Boring (to You) Your character grew up with everbright lanterns, sending stones, and the Lightning Rail. They're as exciting to you as streetlights, phones, and trains. Don't roleplay awe at basic magitech — your character has seen it every day of their life. What would impress them is the rare, high-level stuff: a bound elemental breaking free, a manifest zone surging, an artifact from the Age of Giants. Save your wonder for things that are actually extraordinary in-world. Also: look up at night. Eberron has a planetary ring and twelve moons. Your character has never seen a sky without them.

08 — Dragonmarks

Chapter Summary Dragonmarks are hereditary magical sigils tied to specific bloodlines and races. Each mark grants innate abilities — Making, Healing, Storm, Warding, and more. The marks come in three tiers (Least, Greater, Siberys) and can't be learned or stolen. Aberrant marks are the wild, dangerous exception: unpredictable powers that the houses have historically tried to exterminate.

Dragonmarks are hereditary magical sigils that grant specific powers. Each is tied to a bloodline family that has built a mercantile empire around that mark. They are Eberron's megacorps—more powerful than most nations, technically "neutral" per the Treaty, practically anything but.

What They Are

A dragonmark is a pattern of blue-black lines that appears on the skin, somewhere between a tattoo and a birthmark. They first manifest during adolescence or times of stress—often dramatically. The mark is alive: it shifts subtly, and it grows. A Least mark might cover part of a forearm. A Greater mark can span a shoulder or chest. A Siberys mark—exceedingly rare—covers the entire body like a living map.

Each mark grants innate magical abilities tied to its domain. The Mark of Making lets you mend and fabricate. The Mark of Storm lets you influence wind and weather. The Mark of Healing lets you cure wounds with a touch. These aren't spells you learn—they're abilities that emerge from the mark itself, and they grow more powerful as the mark develops.

Dragonmarks only appear in specific bloodlines tied to specific races. You can't learn one. You can't steal one. You're born with the potential, or you're not. This is what gives the houses their monopoly: nobody else can do what their marked heirs do, at least not as efficiently.

Aberrant Marks

Then there are the aberrant dragonmarks — wild, painful marks that appear from cross-house pairings or at random. They grant unpredictable, often destructive powers: fire that burns the bearer, touch that kills, uncontrolled psychic blasts. The houses fear them. In the War of the Mark, two devastatingly powerful aberrant-marked leaders destroyed parts of Sharn before the united houses crushed them — and then purged every aberrant bearer they could find. The survivors of Breland's subsequent "suicide squad" missions founded House Tarkanan, which isn't really a house at all — it's organised crime with a dragonmark. In the present day, aberrant-marked people are outcasts. Excellent PC material.

Player Note If you take an aberrant dragonmark, expect social consequences. The houses exterminated bearers in the War of the Mark, and that fear hasn't faded. House Tarkanan might be your only allies — and they're criminals.
Player Note — Dragonmarks in 2024 Rules Forge of the Artificer overhauled how dragonmarks work mechanically. Marks are no longer locked to a subspecies — they're backgrounds and feats. Pick a dragonmark background at character creation, or take a dragonmark feat later to represent the mark manifesting in adulthood. Marks evolve through feat chains: Lesser → Greater → Siberys. Any species can bear any mark — lore supports this (the Mark of Finding appears in both humans and half-orcs). One caveat: lesser dragonmark feats assume you're a spellcaster; the expanded spell list only works if you have spellcasting. Martial characters miss half the benefit, which stings.

Sharn, City of Towers

Player Note Sharn is the most likely starting city. It's a vertical metropolis — the higher you go, the richer it gets. The lower you go, the more dangerous.

Sharn in cross-section

Sharn is the default campaign hub. Built in a manifest zone linked to Syrania (the plane of azure sky), the city's towers stretch miles into the air. It's vertically stratified: the higher you climb, the richer it gets. The Cogs at the bottom are industrial hells. The upper plateaus drip with gold and politics. A chase scene can go from a penthouse restaurant in Upper Menthis to the goblin-run markets of Lower Dura in minutes—straight down. Think noir Coruscant.

Skycoaches — small flying gondolas — are the city's taxis. Bridges connect towers hundreds of feet above street level. The city watch barely patrols below the middle wards, and in the Cogs, law enforcement is a punchline. Every district has a flavour: Morgrave University sits in Upper Menthis, the Bazaar of Dura never sleeps, Tavick's Landing is where refugees queue, and the Depths are where people disappear.

Beneath it all: Sharn is built on top of an ancient Dhakaani goblin city. The deeper you dig, the older the ruins get — and the closer you get to things that were sealed away for a reason.

DM Note — Running Sharn Use elevation as tonal shorthand — "you descend three levels" tells players the mood just shifted. Population ~200,000, the largest city on the continent. Every house has an enclave, every conspiracy has a foothold. The key crime conflict: Boromar Clan (halfling mob, old money) vs. Daask (Droaam muscle, pushing in). Your address tells everyone your story — pick a ward at character creation and let it shape who you know.

Karrnath, the Gothic Nation

Cold, bleak, martial. Children learn sword drill before letters. Officers are aristocrats. The chain of command is society — Prussia crossed with Ravenloft. Early in the Last War, famine and plague gutted Karrnath. King Kaius I turned to the Blood of Vol for help. The Blood turned Kaius into a vampire and gave Karrnath the tools to raise undead legions: elite necromantic soldiers in dark armour, tireless, disciplined, devastating. Other nations — especially Thrane — never forgave them.

After Thronehold, Kaius III officially decommissioned the legions and sealed them in fortress vaults. Hidden caches remain throughout the nation, maintained by Blood of Vol priests and the outlawed Order of the Emerald Claw. Nobody knows how many are still down there.

Designer's Intent — Karrnath Isn't Evil Baker's most morally interesting nation. The undead legions are horrific — but Karrnath was starving, and the famine killed more people than the fighting. Kaius took the Blood of Vol's offer because the alternative was extinction. The question the setting asks: what would you do? Baker also placed House Deneith (Mark of Sentinel, the protector/bodyguard mark) here deliberately — to push against the gothic aesthetic.
DM Note — The Kaius Secret Kaius III is Kaius I. The original vampire king has been ruling through puppet successors for over a century. He publicly dismantles the undead program he created while secretly controlling everything. He hates the Blood of Vol for what they made him but can't move against them — they know what he is. Don't drop this early. Let players find it in layers: the regent who knows too much about century-old events, the portrait that looks wrong, the priest who smirks about the royal line.
DM Note — Thrane vs. Karrnath Deepest grudge on the continent. Thrane's Silver Flame considers the undead an abomination. Karrnath sees Thrane's crusaders as fanatics who burned lycanthropes alive and lecture everyone about morality. Both have legitimate points, both have blood on their hands. The border is a cold war. A Thranish paladin and Karrnathi soldier in the same party means the conflict writes itself.
DM Note — Running Karrnath Fog, fortresses, locked doors. NPCs speak in rank and title. The tavern keeper was a sergeant. The shopkeeper lost three sons. The patrol commander keeps undead in the basement "just in case." Some veterans consider the legions heroes; others wake up screaming. Heavy food, strong drink, nobody smiles at strangers.
Player Note — Playing a Karrnathi The undead thing follows you everywhere. Thrane might refuse you service; Breland will crack jokes about skeletons. You were raised to serve, and now the war is over. Figure out what you're serving now.
Player Note — The Blood of Vol Rank-and-file worshippers aren't villains — they believe divinity lies within mortal blood, not in distant gods, and see death as a problem to solve. Most have no idea about Vol the lich-queen or the darker institutional layers. A sincere Blood of Vol believer in a party with a Silver Flame devotee is one of Eberron's best RP dynamics.

Droaam, the Monster Nation

Western Khorvaire, carved from land Breland couldn't hold. Medusas, ogres, harpies, gnolls, minotaurs, trolls — all the "monster manual" species, organised into a functioning state by the Daughters of Sora Kell, three hag sisters who seized power about a decade ago. The Treaty of Thronehold doesn't recognise Droaam. The Daughters don't care. They're building legitimacy through trade, mercenary contracts, and the quiet fact that their nation works.

The three sisters: Sora Katra, a green hag, the diplomat and spymaster — she could talk a dragon into a cage. Sora Maenya, an annis hag, the enforcer — raw physical power that keeps warlords in line. Sora Teraza, a dusk hag, blind prophet — she sees the future and may be reading the Draconic Prophecy itself. Together they're arguably the most competent leadership on the continent.

Designer's Intent — Monsters as Citizens Baker's thesis statement on "evil species." Droaam forces the question: who decides what counts as civilised? The hags are manipulative and dangerous, but they built law and trade where Breland had chaos. The "monster nation" is a mirror held up to the "civilised" ones.
DM Note — Running Droaam The troll bridge-keeper collects tolls and makes small talk. The medusa innkeeper wears a veil out of courtesy. Play the mundanity — the shock for visiting PCs is how normal everything feels. Then remind them Sora Maenya crushed a warlord's skull last Tuesday for breaking trade law, and nobody blinked.
Player Note — From Droaam Your nation doesn't officially exist. The Five Nations see you as a monster first and a person maybe never — but explaining your homeland has laws and trade to a Brelish guard reaching for his sword gets old fast. Chip on shoulder, point to prove.

11 — Factions & Power Blocs

Chapter Summary Factions from street-level gangs to cosmic forces, all stacked on top of each other. The Draconic Prophecy is the big picture: dragons (the Chamber) and fiends (the Lords of Dust) have been playing chess with mortal civilisation for millennia. Below that: the Aurum, the Dreaming Dark, the Emerald Claw, the Dark Lanterns, and more. Eight major factions detailed here, each a campaign engine.

Eberron's factions operate on different scales. Some are mortal organisations jockeying for political advantage. Others are cosmic forces playing games that span millennia. Two of the biggest—the Chamber and the Lords of Dust—are locked in a cold war over the Draconic Prophecy.

The Draconic Prophecy

The Prophecy isn't a book or a prediction. It's a pattern written into reality itself — encoded in the movement of moons, the lines of dragonmarks, the geology of mountains, the timing of births and deaths. Think of it less as prophecy and more as code: a vast system of if/then statements. "If an innocent catches a falling star, then the kingdom of man will fall." Dragons have spent hundreds of thousands of years studying it. They don't fully understand it. Nobody does.

What they do know: the Prophecy describes possible futures, not fixed ones. Certain events—a specific person being in a specific place at a specific time—can tip the Prophecy in one direction or another. The Prophecy doesn't command—it describes conditions. A mark might read: "When the bearer of the Storm mark stands where the bridge of fire fell, the imprisoned voice speaks again." Neither the mark nor the voice have opinions about whether this is good. The players who unwittingly fulfil the condition might never know what they set in motion.

The Great Game — Dragons vs. Fiends

Section Summary Ancient dragons (the Chamber) and ancient fiends (the Lords of Dust) are locked in a cold war over the Draconic Prophecy. Both sides use mortal civilisation as a game board, manipulating events across centuries. Roughly thirty godlike Overlords are imprisoned beneath Eberron — the Lords of Dust want them free, the Chamber wants them sealed. Your party might be a piece in this game and never know it.

The Progenitor myth didn't end when Eberron imprisoned Khyber. The children of those dragons—the true dragons of Argonnessen and the fiends born from Khyber's darkness—have been fighting a cold war over the Prophecy ever since. This is the deepest layer of Eberron's conspiracy stack, and it makes everything else look like local politics.

The Chamber is the dragon faction that actively meddles in mortal affairs. They send agents—often polymorphed into humanoid form—to steer events toward prophetic outcomes they consider "safe." A Chamber dragon might spend decades posing as a university professor, a merchant, or a minor noble, positioning mortal pawns for a single moment fifty years in the future. They are patient, brilliant, and utterly indifferent to the collateral damage their plans cause. If a city has to burn to prevent an Overlord from waking, the Chamber will light the match.

The Lords of Dust are the fiendish counterpart. Rakshasa—ancient, cunning, practically immortal—who survived the Age of Demons and now work to free the Overlords: godlike fiends imprisoned in Khyber at the dawn of the world. Each Overlord embodies a concept—the Rage of War, the Voice in the Darkness, the Keeper of Secrets. They aren't locked in dungeons; they're sealed by the Prophecy itself, bound by specific conditions that the Lords of Dust spend millennia trying to unravel. A rakshasa infiltrator might hold a position of power for centuries, nudging events one careful step at a time toward the moment a seal weakens.

Between these two forces, mortal civilisation is a game board. The Chamber and the Lords of Dust don't fight each other directly—they fight through events, through the mortals who unknowingly fulfil or prevent prophetic conditions. Your party could be the key piece in a gambit that started before humans existed on Khorvaire.

Player Note If a dragon or ancient fiend takes a personal interest in you, the stakes just escalated past politics. You might be a piece in a game that predates civilisation — and neither side will explain the rules. That NPC who's been unusually helpful? Maybe they're a Chamber agent who needs you alive for a prophecy mark. The one who keeps getting in your way? Maybe a rakshasa who needs you to fail.
DM Note — The Overlords There are roughly thirty Overlords imprisoned beneath Eberron—each one a campaign-ending threat if freed. Rak Tulkhesh (the Rage of War) stokes conflict across Khorvaire; every border skirmish feeds him. Sul Khatesh (the Keeper of Secrets) hoards forbidden knowledge; every dark pact strengthens her bonds. Bel Shalor (the Shadow in the Flame) is imprisoned beneath Flamekeep itself—the Silver Flame literally exists to keep him contained. You don't need all thirty. Pick one whose theme matches your campaign, and let its influence seep upward through cultists, manifest zones, and increasingly alarming coincidences. The players should feel the Overlord long before they learn its name.
DM Note — The Conspiracy Stack Eberron's conspiracies nest. Street level: Boromar Clan vs. Daask. Above that: nations spy on each other (Dark Lanterns vs. Royal Eyes). Above that: dragonmark houses play economic chess. Above everything: the Chamber and Lords of Dust manipulate it all for prophetic ends. Your campaign can operate at any layer — or let players slowly discover each layer has a layer above it. The goblin gang war was funded by a house agent, manipulated by a rakshasa, countered by a polymorphed dragon. That realisation is when your campaign becomes unforgettable.

Using Factions Every organisation in this table is a story engine. The Aurum funds black-market expeditions; the Chamber sends you on prophetic errands without explaining why; the Emerald Claw ambushes you because you angered Vol last week. Tie player characters to one of these groups and watch your campaign run itself as their agendas collide.
Designer's Intent — Where the Noir Actually Lives Baker has said it's a mistake to look for noir in every atom of Eberron. The noir isn't in the dungeons or the dragon fights—it's in the dragonmarked houses' slow, quiet dismantling of the monarchies. The short-term quest for gold, the long game of economic power replacing political power—that's where the darkness sits. A DM who loves pulp adventure and hates the bleakness of noir can absolutely find what they want in Eberron (Xen'drik expeditions, airship chases, ancient giant ruins). And a DM who wants cynical backroom deals and moral compromise has the houses, the Aurum, and the Dark Lanterns. The setting holds both tones simultaneously—Baker designed it that way.
Why the Dreaming Dark Suppresses Hope The current age of dreams — il-Lashtavar, the Darkness That Dreams — sustains the quori's existence. If mortals shift to a new age through hope and progress, the current quori die and are replaced by entirely new entities. So the Dreaming Dark crushes revolutions and dampens innovation because optimism literally kills them. It's existential self-preservation, not malice — which makes them far more interesting than "evil psychic aliens."
Changelings — It's Not Deception, It's Identity Many changelings maintain multiple stable identities called personas as cultural practice. "Kel" the dock worker and "Lady Alais" the socialite are both real. The three philosophies (Passers, Becomers, Reality seekers) each say something different about belonging and authenticity.

12 — Religion & the Divine

Player Note Gods in Eberron don't prove they exist. Faith is faith — no divine voice confirms you're right. Clerics get power, but that doesn't mean their theology is correct.

Eberron's gods do not demonstrably intervene. Clerics get power, but nobody has met the Sovereign Host at dinner. This means faith is genuine belief, not confirmed fact—and the setting is better for it.

FaithNatureTension
The Sovereign HostPantheon of nine gods covering every domain — war, crafts, nature, trade, the lot. Most common faith in Khorvaire, woven into daily life the way casual religion is in the real world.Nobody has ever met a Sovereign. Faith is genuine belief, not verified fact. Some priests wonder if they're praying to anything at all.
The Dark SixSix gods rejected from the Sovereign Host — the Mockery, the Fury, the Keeper, and more. Worshipped openly in some cultures, furtively in others. The Silver FlameNot a god but a force — a literal pillar of silver fire in Flamekeep that imprisoned ancient fiends. The Church of the Silver Flame is Eberron's most organised religion, with templars, inquisitors, and political power.The institution is corrupt and fallible, but the Flame itself is real. Zealots burned lycanthropes in purges. Good people serve a flawed church — classic Eberron grey.
The Blood of VolBelieves divinity lies within mortal blood, not in distant gods. Seeks to conquer death through understanding it. Not all of them know what they're actually serving.
Designer's Intent — Faith Without a Phone Line Baker designed Eberron's religion as a deliberate reaction to other D&D settings where you can cast commune and get a direct answer from your god. In Eberron, divine magic works—but it's not proof. A cleric who worships no deity at all can still receive spells; a cleric who actively works against their own church keeps their power too. Baker wanted room for schisms, corruption, and genuine crises of faith—things that make no sense if you can just dial up a deity and ask who's right. He specifically wanted the hardboiled ex-paladin who has to find their way back to belief. Religion in Eberron is meant to feel like religion in the real world: deeply personal, sometimes beautiful, sometimes weaponised, and never provably true.
Two Faces of Undeath Eberron has two completely opposite takes on undeath. The Undying Court of Aerenal uses positive-energy necromancy to preserve revered elven ancestors as luminous, benevolent deathless—the highest honour in Aereni culture. Meanwhile, the Blood of Vol sees undeath as the mortal conquest of death itself, using negative energy and blood magic. Same concept, diametrically opposed philosophies, both internally consistent. A Silver Flame paladin hates both equally. An Aereni elf and a Blood of Vol priest would have the most fascinating argument in any tavern.

Eberron Flavour — Quick Reference

What makes a scene feel like Eberron instead of generic fantasy? Roll a d12, or pick whichever fits the moment.

ElementWhat It Means at the Table
Manifest ZoneA place where Eberron physically overlaps with another plane. In a Shavarath zone weapons feel lighter and aggression spikes; in a Mabar zone light dims and healing falters; in an Irian zone everything glows faintly golden. You don't travel to the plane — the plane leaks into you. Great for making a mundane location suddenly feel alien without leaving the map.
House AgentAn employee of a dragonmarked house. Cannith makes things, Jorasco heals, Orien moves people, Thuranni kills people quietly. An agent showing up means their house wants something here — they'll be polite about it right up until you're in the way.
Magitech BreaksWhat happens when the fantasy industrial revolution has a bad day. The Lightning Rail, sending stones, everbright lanterns, bound elementals — when any of it malfunctions, a loose fire elemental is not a tech support ticket.
Last War ScarThe war ended two years ago and nobody won — it stopped because the Mourning annihilated an entire nation and everyone signed a treaty out of terror. Veterans are bitter, borders contested, old weapons sit in fields waiting to activate.
Aberrant MarkThe wrong kind of dragonmark. Normal marks come through bloodlines and do predictable things. Aberrant marks appear randomly, hurt their bearer, and do unpredictable, often destructive things. House Tarkanan collects people with aberrant marks. Society fears them. An NPC whose mark is "doing too much" is a ticking bomb that also has feelings about it.
Warforged IdentitySentient constructs built as disposable soldiers. The Treaty gave them personhood — but two years of legal rights doesn't erase thirty years of being property. They don't sleep, don't eat, many don't have names. "What am I for now?" is an instant story hook.
Morgrave FindMorgrave University in Sharn funds treasure-hunting expeditions to Xen'drik, the shattered continent where giants once enslaved the elves. Expeditions go in, sometimes come back, often come back changed. "A Morgrave find" means someone dug up something they shouldn't have, and now it's loose in civilisation.
Dreaming DarkThe quori — psychic entities from Dal Quor, the plane of dreams — can't physically enter Eberron, so they possess willing hosts called the Inspired, who rule Sarlona. On Khorvaire they operate through sleeper agents and manipulated dreams. The scary part is subtlety: the NPC acting slightly off might not know they're compromised.
Prophecy FragmentThe Draconic Prophecy isn't a prediction, it's a pattern — written in the land itself, in the movement of moons, in dragonmarks, in events. Dragons spend millennia interpreting it. The Chamber and the Lords of Dust both try to steer it. A prophecy fragment showing up means something cosmically significant is happening here, even if nobody at the table understands it yet.
Trust No OneEberron's two great shapeshifter threats. Changelings are a player race — most just want to live their lives, but some exploit their gift. Rakshasas are ancient fiends who occasionally slip free, take a pleasing form, and play very long games. The person you're talking to might not be the person you think — and in Eberron, that paranoia is the point.
Dragonmark RivalryThe houses maintain a polite fiction of cooperation, but beneath the surface they compete ferociously. Cannith's three factions fight over the house's future. Phiarlan and Thuranni are in a cold war of espionage and assassination. Orien resents Lyrandar for stealing the airship market. Every inter-house tension is a quest hook waiting to happen.
The NewspaperThe Korranberg Chronicle is a real, mass-produced newspaper. Actions have public consequences. If the party causes a scene, someone writes about it. If a noble dies, the headline changes the political landscape. Information travels fast in Eberron, and reputation matters. Let the players see their deeds in print — flattering or otherwise.
DM Note — Play It Cinematic Eberron was designed for action-movie energy. Encourage players to swing from airship rigging, surf a collapsing tower, leap between Lightning Rail cars, and tackle a changeling off a sky-bridge. The setting wants dramatic, over-the-top action sequences. When a player describes something cool, say yes and figure out the DC after. Eberron's tone is pulp—reward audacity.
DM Advice — Reframe Generic Quests The same quest reads completely differently when you dress it in Eberron. "Take a bounty on a monster" becomes an eccentric gentleman explorer hiring the party for an expedition to an uncharted island aboard a state-of-the-art elemental galleon—straight out of King Kong. Or go noir: the monster is picking off victims one by one in Lower Dura, the Watch ignores that part of town, and a lone inquisitive reached out to the party as a last resort. "Clear out a lair" becomes "House Cannith is buying up villages to build factories—clear the last creature from this cave to make room for a highway." The setting is post-war industrial, not medieval. If your quest hook could happen unchanged in the Forgotten Realms, rethink the framing.
DM Advice — Three Lore Drops Per Session Pick three Eberron details and work them into each session. Not lore dumps—moments. One session it's a Hobgoblin gang from Darguun, a Daask medusa policing Malleon's Gate, and a Soldorak dwarf with a symbiotic arm whip. Another session: an experimental warforged model, dragonshard-powered magic items, and an NPC with an aberrant dragonmark. For quieter sessions, drop environmental flavour instead: a skycoach flies past, magelight lanterns cast a distinctive warm glow, an artificer mends a broken wheel at the roadside. The point is to not get overwhelmed trying to explain all of Eberron at once. Show, don't tell. When a lore drop appears, ask whether any character would know about it—let them connect it to their backstory, or roll. Your players "feel" the world one detail at a time. And don't forget the night sky: Eberron has a planetary ring and twelve moons. Describing that overhead is instant setting flavour.
Player Note — Media Touchstones If you want to get into the Eberron headspace, think less Lord of the Rings and more: Indiana Jones, The Mummy, Hellboy, the Robert Downey Jr. Sherlock Holmes films, Fullmetal Alchemist, and the Mistborn Era 2 novels. Pulp adventure, noir mystery, magitech industry—that's the vibe. Your character isn't a medieval peasant who found a sword; they're a war veteran, a private eye, or a corporate operative in a world that runs on magic the way ours runs on electricity.
DM Note — Session Zero Essentials Nail down: dragonmark availability, warforged social status in your campaign, whether the Mourning has a cause (even if players don't know it yet), which nations the PCs call home, and who your group patron is. These choices shape everything.
