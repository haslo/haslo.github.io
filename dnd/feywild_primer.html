01 — What Is the Feywild?

Meta Sources & Colour Coding This primer draws from official D&D books (tagged 4e and 5e / 5.5e), YouTube deep-dives, and a community guide that blends real-world Celtic and European fairy lore with Jim Butcher's Dresden Files novels. Domains of Delight (2021) is treated as the primary 5e Feywild source — where it and older material disagree, DoD's framing is presented as main text and the older version moves to a callout. Butcher drew heavily from the same folklore traditions, so the two are intertwined — but where the guide adds concepts that exist only in the novels (court hierarchy tiers, fey knights, specific Wild Hunt lords), those are tagged Dresden so you know they're not D&D canon. Everything tagged Folklore has roots in real-world fairy traditions.

The Feywild is a parallel plane of wild magic and untamed nature — an echo of the material world turned up to eleven. The laws of science, logic, and reason give way to story, rhyme, and emotion. Enchanted forests stretch endlessly, animals talk, castles perch on mountain spires, and time flows differently depending on where you stand.

It is simultaneously a land of breathtaking beauty and terrible danger. Capricious fey play careless games with mortal lives. Hags place everlasting curses. Fomorian tyrants rule underground empires of cruelty. The one consistency: the Feywild is shaped by the emotions of its creatures. Strong feelings can turn a dead tree living, a clear sky to rain. Residents understand this and use emotion to influence the land — intentionally or not. Even a singing mushroom leading you down a forest path might be herding you towards its poisonous brethren.

4e 5e / 5.5e Time Distortion Time passes differently in the Feywild. Some areas bask in eternal summer; others are frozen in endless winter. Some lie in perpetual dawn; others in a forever sunset. A mortal spending a few days in the Feywild might return home to find that everyone they knew has aged and died — or that only minutes have passed. In 3e's Plane of Fairy the rules were harsher: one day there equalled a week on the material plane, and the lost time caught up with you on return — a long stay could age a traveller to death instantly. 5e softened this to an optional rule that can go in either direction. To make matters worse, creatures leaving the Feywild tend to lose their memories of the visit — experiences become fuzzy, dreamlike, even for fey natives (DC 10 WIS save to retain memories on return, per the DMG optional rule).
HotF p. 6 Taking20 Jorphdan Constructed Chaos DDB · Lore 101
4e Storybook Logic & Fey Rules Fey magic bends and breaks the rules that govern arcane study in the material world. The humblest midwife can curse a mortal for a lifetime. Fey establish borders based on conditions rather than geography — and those conditions are as binding as iron walls. Cold iron is the one material boundary that fey universally respect and fear. Three rules bind the Feywild: No lies — fey have a natural disdain for outright falsehood, but half-truths and omissions are an art form. Binding agreements — even a casual verbal "can do" constitutes a magically enforced contract; failure to honour it causes the land itself to punish you. Reciprocity — accepting a gift (even food when you're hungry) obligates you to return one of equal value, but fey appraise by sentimental or momentary worth, not gold. That fruit might cost you your firstborn.
HotF pp. 7–8 Constructed Chaos
Meta D&D History The Feywild as a named plane didn't exist before 4e (2008). It's a unification of two older concepts: Arboria from 2e Planescape (a three-layered plane with seelie/unseelie fey, forests, and endless water) and the Plane of Fairy from 3e (described as a land of soft lights and cruel desires). 4e merged both into the Feywild and gave it an origin story: when the primordials shaped Abeir-Toril, they found some pieces too vivid and bright, others too murky and dark, and flung them away. The bright fragments clustered into the Feywild; the dark ones became the Shadowfell. 5e carried the plane forward largely unchanged. It's still relatively young in D&D lore, which is partly why so much of it is left deliberately vague — DMs are meant to fill it in.
Taking20 Jorphdan
5e / 5.5e Wild Magic Arcane magic runs stronger throughout the Feywild. Spells can come out stronger, weirder, or have completely different effects than on the material plane. Everything in the Feywild is magical — people, forests, bread. Casting detect magic is useless here because everything pings. This is both the plane's greatest gift and its greatest peril: power without predictability.
PH · Feywild
5e / 5.5e Fey Crossings The most common way to reach the Feywild without plane shift is via a fey crossing — a place where the two planes blur together. Geographical features on one side tend to mirror the other. No two crossings work the same; some operate only at certain times or when certain conditions are met. An archfey is usually aware of any crossings into their domain. DoD provides a d8 table of crossing types — mushroom circles, hollow trees, stone altars, crystal caves, ruined towers, and more.
DoD pp. 17–18 Taking20 PH · Feywild DDB · Lore 101
5e / 5.5e Feywild Weather DoD includes a d8 Feywild Weather table with effects lasting an hour or so — flower-blossom rain, tiny fruit tarts falling from the sky, fog that gives you fog moustaches, or a howling wind that steals trinkets from your pockets.
DoD p. 5
5e / 5.5e Spells in the Feywild Spells take on cosmetic Feywild flavour here — magic missile might manifest as a giggling sprite firing tiny glowing arrows, phantom steed looks like a giant fuzzy caterpillar. DoD provides a short table of examples.
DoD pp. 4–5
5e / 5.5e Domain Borders Domains of Delight can grow or shrink with their archfey's power. Overlapping domains cause territorial disputes; borders can be sealed with shimmering mist, magic barriers, or riddles.
DoD p. 5
4e Many Worlds, One Feywild Whether your campaign is set in the Forgotten Realms, Eberron, or a homebrew world, the Feywild exists as described in these sources with little or no alteration. Its features mirror every world at once — a mysterious property that makes the material setting-agnostic.

02 — The Fey — Alien Neighbors

The fey are the creature type with the most real-world folklore behind them — centuries of Irish, Scottish, Welsh, and broader European tradition. In D&D, they're the most "people-like" of all monster types. They have houses, jobs, a nebulous idea of government. Just as their plane is a twisted mirror of the material world, fey are a weird reflection of the people living in that world. PH · Fey Problem

Fey are associated with neutral alignment — partly because nature in D&D has been neutral since the 1970s, partly because this tracks with folklore portrayals where fairies genuinely could not care less about human morality. PH · Fey Problem

5e / 5.5e Blue-and-Orange Morality This is the core concept. Fey don't operate on a good/evil axis. They have their own moral framework that's completely alien to mortals. They might have equivalents of "good" and "evil" — but those equivalents serve the same structural purpose without being actual equivalents. Their laws, their values, their morals — all different from ours. This isn't randomness — fey have a moral code, they just follow or break a different one. Depicting fey as "lol so random" misses the point entirely. The interesting thing about fey is confronting a completely alien but internally consistent moral framework. PH · Feywild PH · Fey Problem PH · Adventure DDB · Lore 101

This alien morality is what makes interacting with fey always complicated. They follow rules — just not ones mortals can easily understand. This creates interesting moral dilemmas in play and is the key to making fey feel genuinely fey rather than just "nature elves." PH · Feywild

Fey Nature — No Free Will

Folklore No Free Will Fey cannot change their fundamental nature — a predatory fey will always be predatory. Their natures are more fluid than the rigid Laws binding them, but they literally cannot act against their core being. A fey appearing to go against its nature is almost certainly under external influence — compelled by magic, oath, or a power greater than itself. Folk Guide

Content tagged Folklore draws from real-world fey traditions. Content tagged Dresden is from Jim Butcher's Dresden Files novels. Neither is official D&D canon.

The Power of Three

Folklore The Power of Three Three is a binding number in fey tradition. Three queens per court. Hag covens of three. The Mothers may themselves be the Fates of legend. Spells and rituals throughout the Feywild use threes. Significantly, a question asked thrice forces a truthful answer — and when a fey asks the same question three times, it shows they are being genuine rather than trying to trick you through wordplay or omission. Folk Guide

Fey Outlook — Reciprocity, Hospitality, Gifts

DoD codifies the core social rules of fey society into three pillars — the operating system of fey interaction. DoD pp. 6–7

Reciprocity. If something is taken, something of equal value must be given — but fey appraise by sentiment, not gold. Hospitality. Courtesy is expected from both host and visitor; rudeness breaks the bond. DoD includes d8 tables for generating Feywild guides (names, identities, quirks). Gifts. Fey are avid gift-givers; good gifts have sentimental value, throwaway gifts are an insult. Accepting a gift can indebt you — forming an accidental fey contract.

5e / 5.5e Fey Contracts A fey contract forms when a creature receives a gift and is expected to give something in return. You can accidentally enter one by accepting a gift, stealing from a fey, or killing a debtor (inheriting the debt). Lesser contracts break with remove curse; greater ones require wish. DoD provides d8 tables for contract gifts, what the fey wants in return, and penalties for breaking contracts.
DoD pp. 7–9
5e / 5.5e Fey Curses Curses are common punishments among archfey. DoD provides a d8 Fey Curses table and a d12 Ending the Curse table with wonderfully specific rituals (speak the curser's true name three times, get bitten by a faerie dragon of a particular colour, persuade a centaur to carry you for eight miles).
DoD p. 10
5e / 5.5e Fey Abodes DoD's d20 Fey Abodes table is a great encounter generator — a windmill on giant crow's feet, a gingerbread cottage, a walking stone colossus with a tower for a head, a dragon skull in the sand.
DoD p. 11

03 — Seelie & Unseelie

Folklore Origins

Seelie and unseelie are a Scottish classification of the fey, with "seelie" being a much older Middle English term. The classification dates to at least the late 18th century in transcribed form, though the tales are likely much older. Seelie means happy, blessed, lucky. Unseelie means the opposite. It's not good vs. evil — it's more like "less likely to be evil" vs. "almost certainly dangerous." Even seelie fey are so flighty and dangerous that mortals refer to them with compliments ("the fair folk," "the good neighbors") to avoid angering them. Unseelie in the folklore are pretty unanimously portrayed as evil or at minimum never helpful. PH · Fey Problem

The Two Courts: Summer & Gloaming

The fundamental division in fey society is between the Summer Court (Seelie) and the Gloaming Court (Unseelie). The two are not moral opposites — good and evil fey exist in both. Seelie fey cling to civilisation, protocol, and tradition. Unseelie fey indulge primal instincts, shun conformity, and dabble in mysterious magic. Both courts stretch across the Feywild but are by no means the only fey courts — just the most widespread. DoD p. 3 DDB · Lore 101

Meta Summer Court Exclusivity (Not in 5e) Some community sources describe the Summer Court as exclusive: membership limited to pure fey creatures, with courtiers gaining status by hosting parties and presenting gifts to the queen. Those with mixed blood are viewed as repellent — and those rejected by the Summer Court often find a home in the Unseelie court instead. This framing does not appear in the 4e sourcebook (HotF) nor in Domains of Delight. The 5e version makes the Seelie/Unseelie split about values (civilisation vs. instinct) rather than bloodline.
Constructed Chaos

The words themselves come from old Scots/English — "seelie" means happy or blessed, "unseelie" means unhappy or unblessed. Think joyful vs. melancholy rather than good vs. evil. A seelie fey might charm you into dancing until your feet bleed because it's fun. An unseelie fey might shelter you from a storm out of cold obligation. Neither is safe. Tales Arcane One further wrinkle: fey creatures don't necessarily use these terms themselves. "Seelie" and "Unseelie" may be material-plane labels — shorthand mortals use to sort trustworthy-seeming fey from untrustworthy-seeming ones, which is a distinction the fey themselves find irrelevant. Taking20

Meta Edition Tangle — 4e vs. 5e The 5e framing (DoD, WBtW) focuses on Summer Court vs. Gloaming Court as the most prominent courts — but DoD explicitly notes these are "by no means the only great Fey courts." The two are differentiated by temperament (civilisation vs. instinct), not morality. The 4e sourcebook (HotF) doesn't use "Seelie" and "Unseelie" as its organising principle at all — it describes six granular factions (see §04) that don't map neatly onto the two-court model. The Gloaming Fey in 4e are dream-and-twilight creatures, not "the Unseelie court" per se. Note: the Summer Court exclusivity/bloodline framing sometimes attributed to 4e actually comes from community sources (see callout above), not from HotF itself. The 4e factions are richer in granularity; the 5e model is cleaner for play. Pick what serves your table.
Homebrew Deals & Codes — A Framework for Alien Morality Here's a conceptual framework (homebrew, not canon) that operationalizes fey morality: Seelie are bound by deals. Honoring a deal is their moral good; breaking your word is their moral evil. The deal doesn't have to be fair or kind — what matters is whether it's honored. Unseelie are bound by codes. They follow a rigid code of conduct, and their moral good is adherence to that code, their moral evil is breaking it. Even if the code leads to suffering, breaking it is worse. This is blue-and-orange morality made concrete — it lets you create fey that feel genuinely alien rather than just "chaotic neutral." A brownie cleans your house because the deal says so. A code-bound fey protects a sacred grove no matter the cost because the code demands it. Also note: trooping fairies (court-aligned) vs. solitary fairies (Yeats's classification) — courtless fey are seen by both courts as immoral loose cannons. PH · Fey Problem
Homebrew Deals & Codes in Practice Pointy Hat's video proposes two homebrew fey that demonstrate the framework. The Moucheron (Seelie) is a martial fay mercenary paid in blood — it will fight for whoever strikes a deal, but demands a person's worth of blood by sundown, no exceptions. If there aren't enough corpses, the moucheron turns on its employers. The Mamshee (Unseelie) is a fairy godmother bound by the code of unconditional protection — it guards its ward from all harm, forever, including from the ward's own parents or the ward themselves. Mother knows best, to a terrifying degree. The same video's companion adventure Time for Pleasantries puts this moral framework into play — missing children, a fey captor with alien logic, and a final choice with no right answer.
PH · Fey Problem PH · Moucheron PH · Adventure

The Three Laws of the Fey

Folklore describes three ancient Laws binding all fey, regardless of court allegiance. Folk Guide

Folklore Speak No False Words Fey cannot knowingly lie. This is their most binding constraint. However, they deal in half-truths, omission, and implication instead — arts they have perfected over millennia. Even under magical pressure or duress, the truth a fey gives is often more confusing than any lie would be. Expect answers that are technically accurate but actively misleading. Folk Guide
Folklore Break No Oaths Deals and oaths are absolute and binding — magically enforced by the Feywild itself. Accepting a gift constitutes accepting an open-ended deal. Fey genuinely hate being given gifts because it puts them in debt. The only safe time to accept fey food, drink, or gifts is as a formally greeted guest in their dwelling, where the rules of hospitality and guest rights protect you. Folk Guide
Folklore Harm No Outsiders A fey belonging to a Court cannot directly harm someone who isn't ranked lower in the fey hierarchy or who hasn't slighted them. Duels between equals and open war between Courts are explicit exceptions. This is why predatory fey go to such lengths to provoke slights or trick prey into deals — they must justify their violence within the constraints of the Law. Folk Guide

Court Hierarchy

Dresden Court Hierarchy

Fey courts are not simple monarchies. Each court has three female rulers arranged in generational tiers, and the titles themselves may hold more power than the individuals who hold them. Folk Guide

The Mothers (the queen that was) are ancient beyond reckoning — possibly the Fates themselves. Mother Summer is a kindly grandmother figure presiding with warmth and wisdom. Mother Winter is an ugly crone who forces supplicants to prove themselves through trials and suffering. The Mothers are near-omniscient, less manipulative than other fey, and rarely take direct action — their influence works through patience and inevitability. Folk Guide

The Queens (the queen that is) are the active rulers. Titania of Summer is jealous and protective, passionately engaged in court politics. Mab of Winter is coldly logical, calculating, barely visible until she acts — then her will reshapes reality. The Queens answer to the Mothers but hold practical sovereignty. Folk Guide

The Ladies (the queen that will be) are queens-in-training. Aurora of Summer embodies kindness and healing, groomed to eventually replace Titania. Maeve of Winter is cruel and manipulative, learning governance through cruelty. When a tier dies or abdication occurs, everyone moves up and a new Lady is selected. Notably, the titles seem to shape personality over time — suggesting the titles themselves, not the individual holders, are the true rulers of each court. Folk Guide This hierarchy is from Jim Butcher's Dresden Files and is not official D&D canon — Aurora and Maeve are Dresden Files characters.

05 — Fey Weaknesses

Folklore preserves three material weaknesses that affect fey across all courts and factions. These are not merely vulnerabilities — they are primal forces that constrain fey power at the most fundamental level. Folk Guide

Iron

Folklore Iron Cold iron burns fey on contact — not like normal fire, but with a searing, primal agony. Iron death is one of the most agonizingly painful ways for a fey to perish. Iron also disrupts Fey Ways and crossing gates, breaking the threads of magic that connect the planes. Most fey take extraordinary precautions to avoid iron entirely. Hags and redcaps are notable exceptions: hags craft iron teeth and nails as weapons, and redcaps wear iron boots — but even their immunity is hard-won and unnatural. Archfey are not immune to iron — it won't kill them but causes extreme pain and diminishes their power when touched. Folk Guide

Running Water

Folklore Running Water Moving water — rivers, streams, waterfalls, rapids — strips fey of their powers temporarily. Most fey (with the notable exception of aquatic types like merfolk and naiads) cannot cross running water without losing their abilities entirely until they reach the other side. Flying fey are less affected than ground-based ones because they can stay aloft. Archfey can manage crossing with reduced power, but they still suffer diminishment. Rain, being stagnant in collection, has only a milder dampening effect — and notably, rain does not fall naturally in the Feywild, making this a minor concern for native fey. Folk Guide

Thresholds

Folklore Thresholds The threshold of a home — the symbolic and sometimes physical boundary between inside and outside — is a real barrier to fey. Fey cannot freely enter a dwelling uninvited; they must be explicitly invited across. This is why predatory fey work so hard to lure victims outside or trick them into extending invitations — the home itself becomes an ally against them. Folk Guide
Dresden Thresholds — Power Mechanics In Dresden Files canon, threshold strength depends on the life lived inside: a happy family home has a strong threshold; a lonely or temporary dwelling has a weak one. Churches with devout congregations have powerful thresholds that can repel even archfey. Fey lose a fraction of their power crossing an uninvited threshold, with that power restored when they leave. Folk Guide

06 — Fey Creatures & Species

What specific fey can you encounter or play as? The range is vast — from the terrifying hags to the adorable blink dogs, from the seasonal eladrin to the tiny fairies.

Hags

Fey in a way that reminds you why people were scared of the fey in the first place. Immortal evil grandmas who delight in suffering. Their thing: they don't fight if they can sell you a bargain instead — a deal twisted to create maximum suffering. Hags are smart, scheming, patient. They amass secrets and information, making it tempting to enter their awful deals. If they do fight, they're loaded with annoying spells and escape routes. When three gather, they form a coven and unlock devastating lair actions. The storytelling opportunities hags offer are unmatched. Different types for different vibes. The focus on deals and contracts makes hags the most "fey" of all fey monsters. PH · Fey Problem → Deep dive in §08 — Hags

Dryads

Tree-bound fey from Greek mythology (originally called hamadryads; dryads of apple trees are called epimeliads, laurel trees are daphne). In D&D they protect their bonded tree/plant and can put up a small fight (CR 1). The 2024 Monster Manual gave them a table for what their tree might be, but otherwise they're underdeveloped — "they vibe, they do Lorax activities." Potential for more: different types based on tree species, a mother dryad that IS an entire forest, invasive plant dryads. PH · Fey Problem

Blink Dogs

Fey dogs that teleport ("blink"). 40 ft speed + teleportation = extremely mobile. Surprisingly smart (INT 10 — average human intelligence). A staple of civilized fey society, especially among centaurs. Legendary rivalry with displacer beasts — both are blurry/blinky, but one's a dog and one's a cat. In 3.5e, they had their own language. Best used as pets/companions rather than combat encounters. PH · Fey Problem

Eladrin

The "humans of the Feywild" — technically elves but deeply changed by fey influence. Their biology is tied to emotion: skin tone, hair colour, and markings shift to match a seasonal mood. Spring = happy (green, flowers, can fey step an ally). Summer = angry (golden, fey step does fire damage). Autumn = empathetic (warm tones, charms nearby creatures). Winter = melancholy (pale, frightens a creature). Some eladrin stay in one season forever; others shift daily. A great example of how the Feywild changes its inhabitants. PH · Feywild DDB · Lore 101

5e / 5.5e Eladrin — Primal Elves Of all elves, eladrin are closest in form and ability to the first generation of elves created by Corellon. They're proud of it, too — haughty around other elves, though some trade that pride for a tender desire to share the wonders of the Feywild with their cousins. Eladrin culture is the oldest elven civilization and also the most decadent: fickle, impetuous, with a justice system that swings between capriciously harsh and whimsically mild depending on the mood of whoever is passing judgment. Their cities blend towers, arching bridges, streams, gardens, and magic-infused architecture in ways that feel both constructed and completely natural. Prolonged exposure to the Feywild accelerates the onset of Remembrance (the elven past-life memory) in other elves — but eladrin themselves are unaffected. Uniquely among elves, eladrin feel little pull toward Arvandor; they'd rather stay in the Feywild than rejoin Corellon.
MToF pp. 50–51

Sidhe

Folklore Sidhe The lords and ladies of the Seelie and Unseelie courts — the true nobility of the Feywild. Sidhe appear as humanoids of supernatural grace and beauty (or terrible otherworldly otherness for darker variants). Deeply magical, they cast spells as naturally as breathing, see through any illusion with ease, and are nearly immune to charm effects. Each sidhe possesses unique magical talents reflecting their court alignment. Sidhe are obsessed with status and constantly scheming for their Queen's favor. Banished sidhe either join the Wild Hunt, retreat to the wilderness as solitary fey, or flee to the material plane where they often masquerade as nobility or sorcerers. Folk Guide

Korreds

Folklore Korreds Hair-covered, hooved craftsmen shorter than gnomes — masters of construction and artifice. They rule Svartalfheim, one of the few territories in the Feywild that neither Summer nor Winter courts fully control. Korred constructs and fortifications are legendary, built to last forever and defended by iron weaponry — a material that would kill ordinary fey. Even the Queens do not challenge Korred sovereignty. Unlike most fey, Korreds operate on currency and commerce rather than deals and oaths. They are pragmatic traders, willing to negotiate with anyone. Folk Guide

Summer Fey

Folklore Summer Fey

The Seelie summer court draws fey aligned with beauty, joy, and natural abundance. Faerie dragons are playful illusionists and tricksters, small but magically formidable, delighting in chaos more than malice. Gruffs are goat-folk shock troopers standing four feet tall with curved horns and muscular frames — they fight as tight-knit family units, with each family group coordinating perfectly in combat. Treants are awakened tree guardians, ancient and slow to move but immensely strong, tragically vulnerable to necrotic magic and requiring decades to fully heal from serious injury. Unicorns are rare court guardians, creatures of purity and healing — killing one brings a generational curse. Folk Guide

Winter Fey

Folklore Winter Fey

The Unseelie winter court draws fey aligned with shadow, predation, and cold calculation. Leanansidhe are beautiful handmaidens with disconcerting cat's eyes, talented in magic and seduction — they are predators who hunt through charm and manipulation. Redcaps are cursed creatures that must soak their hats in blood to survive; their iron boots slowly drain their life force, making their existence short and agonizing — they are born bitter and angry. Quicklings are tiny fey, impossibly fast, with attention spans to match — they are often employed as messengers or spies by the Winter Queen. Yeth hounds are dog-like creatures with disturbing humanoid faces, the Winter Court's equivalent to Seelie pegasi — they fly and hunt in packs, relentless and howling. Folk Guide

08 — Hags

Hags deserve their own chapter because they're the quintessential fey villains — and arguably the most "fey" creatures in the entire Monster Manual. Everything that makes fey dangerous (deals, manipulation, patience, alien morality) is dialled up to eleven in a hag. They don't just live in the Feywild; they embody its darkest logic. (Builds on the overview in §06 — Fey Creatures.)

Folklore The Anti-Mother & Folklore Roots The hag is the anti-mother — the dark mirror of the mother archetype. Where a mother represents creation, nurturing, and warmth, the hag embodies death, consumption, and corruption. As fey, hags don't operate on the logic of physics but of stories and emotion: a green hag doesn't live in a swamp because she likes the smell — she is the rot. Her reason for being is to find someone pure and prove that under the right pressure, they're just as selfish and cruel as she is. Spiritual ugliness, not just physical.

The D&D coven traces back to the Graeae of Greek myth — three sisters who shared a single eye and a single tooth, stronger together than apart. Black Annis from English folklore — a blue-faced crone with iron claws who lived in a cave and snatched children at night — is the direct ancestor of the annis hag. And the Brothers Grimm gave us the cardinal hag rule: the gingerbread house. A hag makes something so inviting that the victim walks in of their own free will. That principle should underpin every hag encounter you run.

Covens

Three hags of any type can form a coven — and everything changes. A coven shares a single pool of powerful magic (effectively an 11th-level spellcaster), gains lair actions, and becomes a genuine campaign-level threat. The hags don't need to like each other; covens are business arrangements, full of backstabbing, paranoia, and contingency plans. Breaking a coven means killing or separating one member — the other two lose their shared magic instantly. Smart players figure this out. Smarter hags have a backup third.

5e / 5.5e Auntie & Grandmother Hags Gristlecracker's introduces a hag hierarchy beyond the Monster Manual baseline. Auntie hags are experienced hags who've survived long enough to mentor younger ones — they're stronger, craftier, and have accumulated more magic (typically CR 8–12). Grandmother hags are ancient, near-mythic figures who've been scheming for centuries — they sit at the top of hag society and can challenge high-level parties (CR 13+). Each standard hag type gets auntie and grandmother variants with expanded abilities.
Gristlecracker's
5e / 5.5e Eberron — The Daughters of Sora Kell The most powerful coven in any D&D setting rules an entire nation. Sora Katra (green hag, spymaster), Sora Maenya (annis hag, warlord), and Sora Teraza (dusk hag, oracle) are the Daughters of Sora Kell — children of the legendary night hag Sora Kell, who has wandered the planes for tens of thousands of years. In 986 YK, Teraza united her sisters to seize Droaam, a monster nation carved from western Breland. They're hags "in the same way that Bahamut is a dragon" — archfey-tier power (CR low 20s). Their coven grants a shared consciousness network spanning a mile: thoughts, senses, and emotions merge instantly, making coordinated strategy effortless and ambush nearly impossible. Droaam has since become the greatest concentration of hags in Eberron, with other hags migrating there to serve under the Daughters' rule.

Cegilune

Most hags worship — or at least pay lip service to — Cegilune, the hag goddess. She grants her followers lesser immortality: a hag doesn't age as long as she keeps up the worship. Lose Cegilune's favour and the centuries catch up fast. Hag lairs almost always have a shrine — a well-maintained cauldron exposed to moonlight at least once a week, never used for cooking. Hags are deathly afraid of solar eclipses (Cegilune at her weakest) and will lock themselves away until the eclipse passes. Gristlecracker's

Hag Lairs

Every hag lair has its own character, but common features include: a weird garden of unsettling plants that makes perfect sense only to its tender, a death yard where a buried cursed object corrupts the land in a radius, and at least a few expendable minions — brutes, familiars, slaves, or warped creatures. Urban hags hide in basements, sewers, and condemned buildings; wilderness hags take swamps, caves, and ruins. The one constant: layers of defence designed to let the hag escape while intruders die slowly. Gristlecracker's

Hags & the Feywild

Here's the twist: most hags don't actually like the Feywild. The plants and supernatural ingredients they rely on can't be grown there, and everything they want to corrupt lives on the Material Plane. Foreign-born hags (those not born in the Feywild) see living there as exile. But for a hag on the run from witch hunters or adventurers, the Feywild is the one place where pursuit is genuinely dangerous for the pursuers. The fey themselves despise a foreign hag neighbour — but they'll hinder anyone foolish enough to invade the Feywild chasing one. Gristlecracker's

Meta Running Hags — DM Advice Five common mistakes and how to fix them. (1) Solo hags die in two rounds. A CR 3 green hag vs. a level 5 party loses to action economy every time. Fix: minions (redcaps, charmed villagers, blighted plants), cursed lair items, and healing mechanics — BG3's Auntie Ethel is the masterclass. (2) Coven clustering = fireball bait. The 30 ft range requirement puts three hags in one AoE; kill one and the other two lose shared spells instantly. Fix: spread them across the lair (entrance, deeper in, balcony/pit), and make killing the first one feel like a trap — the surviving two get angry and the lair reconfigures. (3) Cornered hags can't escape. Give every hag an exit: trap doors, tree-swap lair actions, green hag invisible passage, night hag etherealness used aggressively. (4) Stat block tunnel vision. A 400-year-old creature should have centuries of cursed trinkets, stolen magic, and alchemical weirdness — not just claw/claw. Place at least three items in the lair that aren't in any rule book. (5) Rolling initiative too early. A hag's most powerful weapon costs zero spell slots: her words. Design every encounter so combat is the worst option. "Kill me and the boy in the cellar starves."

The bargain formula: identify what the party needs → the hag offers an instant fix, cheerfully → the price sounds worthless ("your last breath" = auto-fail first death save; "your shadow" = disadvantage on CHA checks vs. good-aligned creatures who notice). Every accepted bargain plants a plot hook directly into the character sheet.
Flamey

Baba Yaga

The most famous hag in any mythology or game system. In Slavic folklore, Baba Yaga lives in a hut that walks on chicken legs, surrounded by a fence of human skulls. She flies in a mortar, steers with a pestle, and sweeps her tracks with a broom. Three mystical horsemen serve her — White (Dawn), Red (Sun), and Black (Night) — marking the passage of time around her domain. She is not evil, exactly: she tests those who seek her out, sets impossible tasks, and rewards cleverness with aid and magic. Fail or cheat, and she eats you. The folklore version is a threshold guardian — you cannot complete your quest without her, but surviving the encounter is itself a trial.

In D&D, she's officially CR 20 (WBtW) — true neutral, something beyond a hag entirely. Gristlecracker's bumps her to CR 22 and frames her as a force of nature rather than an evil grandmother: druids and rangers see her as an eccentric nature spirit. Her bargains have no hidden traps but are exact, terrible, and powerful. Break one and she becomes a relentless nemesis who ignores political borders and moral boundaries. If destroyed, she reforms at the centre of a natural disaster — forest fire, flood, hailstorm. Her hut walks on three massive chicken legs (sentient, loyal, telepathically linked to her). She can shapechange into any creature her CR or lower; her true form is a withered green hag with dull gray skin. WBtW Gristlecracker's

09 — The Wild Hunt

Folklore The Wild Hunt One of the most feared phenomena in the Feywild and material world alike. The Wild Hunt rides during the Feast of the Moon — a cosmic event where the boundaries between planes thin and hunting becomes irresistible. No creature can stop or redirect the Hunt once it begins; they can only flee or join it. Folk Guide

The Three Lords

Dresden The Three Lords

Three archfey lead the Hunt, each with terrifying power. The Erlking is the true leader — a nine-foot-tall archfey with magnificent antlers, wielding a greatsword, wrapped in the hide of an owlbear. He commands respect through overwhelming magical and martial prowess. Vadderung is a mysterious one-eyed figure whose true nature remains unclear — some scholars believe he is an angel or even a god of hunting, transcending fey altogether. Vold is an ogre-sized gremlin riding a shadow-wolf, hurling barbed spears with deadly accuracy and laughing at the chaos. The three balance each other's temperament — Erlking leads, Vadderung moderates, Vold drives the frenzy forward. Folk Guide The Wild Hunt itself is real European folklore, but these three lords (Erlking, Vadderung=Odin, Vold) are Jim Butcher's characterization in the Dresden Files.

Hunting and Joining

Folklore Hunting and Joining The Wild Hunt is an unstoppable force of predatory fey and monsters driven by the urge to hunt. Predatory creatures from across the planes can volunteer to join the Hunt, drawn by the primal compulsion to chase prey. The hunt itself is relentless and predatory by nature. Folk Guide
Dresden Hunting and Joining — Leadership Challenge In Dresden Files canon, a powerful mortal or monster can seize leadership of the entire Hunt by defeating the current leader while riding — though such a victory typically comes at the cost of the victor's life or sanity. Huntsmen wield special weapons forged by the Erlking that inspire bloodlust in their wielders and summon spectral armor that grants protection. Folk Guide

11 — Hyrsam, Prince of Fools

Hyrsam — Prince of Satyrs
The Satyr Prince Hyrsam, he sings
Of times more than mere story—
Before gods or masters or kings,
Before death, fate, or glory. — Traditional Faerie Song Dragon 422 p. 4

Who He Is

Hyrsam is one of the most ancient fey creatures in existence — the eldest son of Oran the Green Lord. He remembers the earliest days of the Feywild, before Corellon and the other gods discovered it, before the fomorians held dominion, before elves, eladrin, or drow existed. In those primordial days the Feywild was a brutal but beautiful realm where gnomes, satyrs, dryads, and treants frolicked unfettered by the structures of court or church. Dragon 422 p. 4

The Mask

In public, Hyrsam plays the consummate bon vivant — the Court of Stars' resident jester, travelling from court to court with his entourage of musicians and minstrels, entertaining fey nobles. He oscillates between comedy and savagery, and the fool act is entirely deliberate. He is both clever and erudite, with a deep understanding of how to manipulate others. His father Oran actively refutes rumours about Hyrsam's involvement in the fall of various kingdoms — but only because Hyrsam is careful enough to keep his hands clean. Dragon 422 p. 4

His True Goal

Hyrsam is a fey anarchist. He seeks the complete collapse of all fey kingdoms so that the Feywild returns to its original pristine state — wild, untamed, and free of the "corruption" of civilisation, Seldarine influence, and fomorian tyranny alike. His songs sow the seeds of rebellion wherever he goes. His revelers offer clandestine assistance to rebels and traitors. Many kingdoms have collapsed in the wake of his visits. Dragon 422 pp. 4–6

Homebrew Twinkle Connection Twinkle studied Hyrsam's domain academically — chaos magic, how his influence shapes the Feywild, how disorder and order coexist in the Weave. She wasn't a worshipper; she was a researcher. Then the chaos she was studying decided to become part of her. Wild Magic found her, or Hyrsam noticed her and thought it would be funny.

Appearance

A handsome satyr with curly locks that fall in wild tangles around curved spiral horns. His dark eyes flash with hidden passion and rebellious light. He dresses in ordinary travelling clothes among commoners and in regal finery among nobles — always adapting to his audience. Dragon 422 p. 4

His Court — The Revelers

Hyrsam's court is a travelling menagerie. Satyrs and their dark cousins (satyrs of the night) form the majority, but his retinue also includes dryads, lamia, eladrin, drow, gnomes, fomorians, and outsiders from distant planes — renegade genasi, githyanki, dwarves, humans, halflings. He welcomes everybody, regardless of race or alignment. The only requirements: a sincere love of the Feywild, a talent for music or storytelling, and a willingness to set aside the prejudices of your former life. Dragon 422 p. 5

Revelers pledge their lives to protect the Satyr Prince. Their hair is tousled, their eyes have a manic gleam, and magical music surrounds them even when they're not playing. They can suppress this effect when stealth is needed. CHA 18+ required — Hyrsam can boost a recruit's Charisma if they fall short. Dragon 422 p. 6

The Grove of Laughing Goats

Hyrsam's home base is a gargantuan tree with eight wooden platforms strung between its branches — glowing lanterns, never-ending kegs of wine, and tents for resting between days of partying. The grove moves with him. It is less a court and more a perpetual festival that happens to have a political agenda.

Immortality

Legend holds that Hyrsam was born from the very first notes of music. He is effectively immortal — if killed, he reappears elsewhere in the Feywild at the next dawn or dusk. The only way to ensure his permanent death: deafen him at the moment he dies. Dragon 422 p. 5

Hyrsam, Savage

The jester has a darker side. Hyrsam remembers the Feywild as a place of savage beauty — when legendary beasts roamed freely before the fomorians and gods drove them to the farthest reaches. He knows the songs that can soothe and control these ancient creatures, and occasionally unleashes them on unsuspecting castles and fortifications, reducing them to rubble. Dragon 422 p. 5

Encounter Hooks

Dragon 422 provides several adventure hooks for incorporating Hyrsam into a campaign: as a revolutionary fomenting rebellion, as a thief who stole Tiandra's crown and fled into the Maze of Fathaghn (forcing the party to broker a deal), or as an unlikely companion against a mutual enemy. The article also includes backstory suggestions for player characters with Hyrsam connections — protégé, displaced heir, believer who heard his songs as a child. Dragon 422 pp. 5–6

12 — Real-World Folklore

D&D's Feywild draws from centuries of Celtic and European fairy traditions — but flattens them into a single setting. Knowing the originals makes the Feywild richer at the table, because the real folklore is weirder, darker, and more specific than anything WotC has published.

The Seelie/Unseelie courts are Scottish, not Irish. In Ireland, the sidhe (pronounced "shee") — the aes sídhe, people of the hollow mounds — don't split into good and bad courts. Yeats divided Irish fairies into trooping (social, court-dwelling, fond of music and processions) and solitary (loners — the banshee, the leprechaun, the dullahan). The two systems don't map onto each other neatly, and D&D's binary Seelie/Unseelie is a simplification of the Scottish tradition alone.

Welsh fairies are a separate taxonomy entirely. Wirt Sikes catalogued the Tylwyth Teg into five types: Ellyllon (elves), Bwbachod (household brownies), Coblynau (mine spirits), Gwragedd Annwn (lake maidens), and Gwyllion (mountain hags). The Mabinogion — the core Welsh mythology text — contains otherworld journeys and fairy encounters that feel nothing like the Irish or Scottish traditions.

Common threads across all Celtic fairy lore: iron as a ward, the danger of eating fairy food, the binding power of names and promises, time flowing differently in fairy realms, and the absolute seriousness of hospitality obligations. These are the bones that D&D, Dresden Files, and every modern fantasy treatment built on.

Key primary sources are listed below. Most are free online. Robert Kirk's Secret Commonwealth (1691) reads like a DM's field guide written by someone who believed every word of it.
